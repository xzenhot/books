In the mythology of the Manasamangal, when Lakshindar dies from the bite of the snake sent by the goddess Manasa[1], family members and society expected Behula to do what every traditional widow was supposed to do: mourn, perform the last rites, burn or bury the body, and accept her fate.
Yet, Behula refused to leave his body[1]. Her refusal is rooted in several deeply symbolic, emotional, and spiritual reasons:
A Love That Defied Mortality: To Behula, death was not a logical conclusion to love; it was an interruption. Her bond with Lakshindar (who, according to the myth, was her celestial partner from a past life[2]) transcended earthly rules. Leaving him would mean accepting that death was stronger than her love—a premise she utterly rejected.
Refusal of Passive Victimhood: If she had accepted the death and stayed behind, she would have surrendered to the wrath of Goddess Manasa and the cruel decree of fate. By climbing onto the banana-raft (bhela) with his corpse[1], she turned grief into an active rebellion. She chose to fight the heavens rather than submit to the societal script written for widows.
The Unshakable Oath of a Wife: In that era, the marriage vow (sindoor and sacred ties) was looked upon as an eternal spiritual pact. For Behula, her duty as a wife did not expire when her husband's heartbeat stopped. If he was going into the unknown realm of death, she was determined to accompany him or bring him back.
A Quest for Justice, Not Just Mourning: Behula understood that her husband’s death was a murder born out of a divine ego clash between her stubborn father-in-law (Chand Sadagar) and the serpent goddess Manasa[1]. Lakshindar was an innocent victim. Leaving his body behind would mean letting injustice win. She stayed with him because she was determined to take her case directly to the court of the gods (Indra’s court)[3] and demand an explanation.
