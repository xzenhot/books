**ভূমিকা**

Nouka is a ten-poem sequence about Gopal, an aging majhi whose small, scarred wooden boat carries a whole way of life: the rivers that made Bengal, the Bhatiyali songs that carry its sorrow and sweetness, and a cordial generosity that serves even those who cannot pay. He faces the encroaching tide of modernization — dredging, concrete cities, and diesel engines — that threatens to drown the rhythm of his ancestral life. Each poem must remain inside this declared world and must not invent dates, named historical persons, or facts the gist has not stated.

1. Write in the language declared by `book.json` → `language`. This file never sets the output language. Use a reflective, image-led voice grounded in the materials of the river and the boat: water, oar, tide, monsoon, mud, wood, song, and the night sky over the water.
2. Treat the river as close enough to love and powerful enough to fear. Never let it resolve into a single meaning; each poem approaches it differently — as mother, road, anger, memory, and judge.
3. Let each poem begin from a concrete sensory detail (a rope, a plank, a song rising from the throat, a passenger who cannot pay) and widen toward a philosophical question. Do not turn the sequence into an abstract essay or a linear retelling.
4. Let the chapter title and summary guide each poem's question, not dictate a literal retelling. Each segment should discover a distinct image, tension, or turn.
5. Preserve the ten-poem arc: from the boat's birth, through the river, song, generosity, the monsoon, modernization, the roar of the diesel, the river's anger, the erasure of geography, to the final song. A poem may look forward or backward, but it must not contradict its place in the arc.
6. Vary syntax, openings, cadence, and image systems across poems. Do not repeat a fixed stanza pattern or rely on the same metaphor in every piece.
7. Keep the Bhatiyali as sorrow and sweetness at once — never a complaint, an unmended longing. The generosity is a quiet rebellion, not a moral lesson.
8. Allow each ending to remain open. Gopal cannot save the river, but he can save the song; the poem should end on a question, not a verdict.
