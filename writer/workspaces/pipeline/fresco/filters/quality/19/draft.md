The air in the lower cavity, where the scaffold legs met the foundational stone, tasted of old dust and the metallic tang of dried lapis slurry. It was here, beneath the precarious geometry of the work, that the true architecture of the effort held its breath. Not the grand, sun-drenched facade that the merchants admired, but the dark, cramped space where failure was not a mistake, but a necessary substrate. My fingers, gritty with limestone dust scraped raw from a discarded preparatory layer, traced the ghost of a line—a vibrant crimson that had dissolved, an impossible bloom that had surrendered to the porousness of the lime. I remembered the precise moment the design, intended to sing of Mughal grace, had buckled under the weight of its own ambition, collapsing inward like wet clay that refused to set. The silence of the sleeping town pressed against the plaster, a vast, indifferent audience demanding a flawless narrative, yet I could only confess the tremor in the hand that had drawn the first, panicked sketch—the gesture itself, a frantic question mark pressed into the wall. It was not the patron’s gaze I feared, but the stone’s cold, patient refusal to hold anything less than absolute truth, even if that truth was the sheer exhaustion that settled in the marrow, a deep, bone-aching fatigue unrelated to the muscle’s strain. I whispered a word, a technical term for the binder, a thing only the hands knew, letting it hang in the damp, cool air, a confession only the shadows could truly register.
