# The Night Shift

The town sleeps behind its walls while the painter works, and this is not solitude but a detachment, a chosen distance from every warm and sleeping thing, bought night after night and paid for in the silence that now magnifies the smallest sound of his own moving. The tallow lamp burns low on the scaffold, throwing its orange heat against the plaster, and between its small circle of light and the vast dark beyond there is the whole truth of the labor: what the flame reveals is the making, and what the dark hides is the making of the man. He grinds pigment on the stone, the lapis and the copper oxide, and a metallic tang rises from the powder to settle on his tongue, the taste of a thing that will be color only after his hands have broken it down.

He mixes the lime with water and the sound of it is the only music in the night, a slow grinding that becomes a kind of prayer, a purification repeated until the wrist aches and the motion passes beyond thought into the trance of the body. The bone tool scrapes against coarse plaster and the sound is abrasive and honest, the voice of work that no one will ever hear. Why do I labor only in the dark, he asks, and the dark does not answer, and the question itself is older than the lamp, older than the pigment, a question the night has been asking every maker since the first hand lifted toward a wall.

He remembers, in the stillness, a conversation with a collaborator who is gone now, a voice that once argued with him about the nature of truth in a color, and the memory arrives like a draft through the alcove, carrying the faint scent of a domestic life behind the walls, a cooking fire, a sleeping child, a life that continues while his own narrows to this circle of flame. He understands that the night is not romantic; it is a tax he pays so that the town may wake to praise a beauty it will never watch being born. The lamp gutters, and for a moment the dark presses in from every side, and he feels the inky cold settling into the pores of his hands, the cold that no pigment can warm.

He works on, because the pattern requires a continuity that only the night can give, a line that must run unbroken from one darkness into the next, and he knows that progress and stagnation have blurred into one long, repeating hour in which he is always grinding, always mixing, always scraping, and never quite arriving. The lamp burns lower, and he lets it, watching the flame consume its fuel the way the work consumes his nights, and he thinks that this is the cost of the blossom that the town will call beautiful, the hidden shift, the unseen hand, the name that will never be written on the wall but which the wall, in its patient darkness, is already holding close.
