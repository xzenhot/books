# The Wasted Sketch

No one ever sees the wasted sketches. They pile in the corner of the workshop like fallen leaves — the charcoal studies, the botched hands, the faces that would not come right, the processions that got only as far as the third figure before he crumpled them and began again.

The apprentice once asked, sweeping the pile, whether it was not a sin to spend so many hours on drawings that would never be shown. The painter did not answer at first. Then he said: the finished wall is not the work. The finished wall is only the last of the work. Every blossom grows out of a grave, he said, and the grave is full of everything that was tried and failed and thrown away so that the one line that survived could survive.

And it is true, he thinks, watching the apprentice carry the crumpled sheets away. The waste is not waste. It is the cost, exact and unsparing, that the blossom never shows — the rehearsals, the false starts, the hundred attempts that taught the hand how to make the one that would be kept. The town will see the wall and think it sprang whole from nothing. Only the painter, and the pile of discarded sketches, and the floor that has swallowed so much charcoal, will know what it actually cost to make a thing look effortless.
