# The Lime

The lime is the craft's one cruelty, and the painter respects it the way a man respects a flame. Slaked fresh, it eats whatever it touches — the plaster, the pigment, the soft skin between his knuckles — and it does not discriminate between the wall and the hand that feeds it.

He has learned to work fast, because the lime will not wait. It sets as it dries, sealing the color into the plaster before the hand is ready, so that every stroke is a race against the material that both carries the painting and consumes the painter. At the end of a long night his knuckles are raw and whitened, the skin dissolved in fine, slow burns that will take a week to heal and will never heal all the way. The lime does not hate him. It is only doing what lime does. But the cost is written on his hands in a language only another mason would read.

He binds the burns with a rag and goes on, because that is the bargain at the heart of the whole thing: the wall will be beautiful, and the hand will be ruined, and the two facts are the same fact seen from two sides. The lime holds the painting together, and the lime undoes the painter, and when he is gone the wall will still be standing, and somewhere in its plaster, mixed in with the lime and the pigment, will be a little of the man who fed it — which is, he thinks, the only way a maker ever truly enters the thing he made.
