The dust, fine as ground gypsum, clung to the boy’s knuckles, a residue of countless hours spent grinding the earth pigments, and in the sputtering light of the kerosene lamp, his question hung heavier than the dried ultramarine dust in the mortar. He traced the unpainted curve of the niche with a fingertip, the gesture mirroring the hesitant breath he held, a silent inquiry into the vanishing point of the self. Why, he whispered, when the ochre skin of the wall held the weight of a hundred sleepless nights, did the signature never bloom? The master, his shoulders already bowed by the scaffold’s geometry, did not look up from the meticulous sharpening of the boar-bristle brush, the high, thin whine of the whetstone slicing against the stone a sudden, desperate counterpoint to the boy’s innocent disruption. He began, instead, to grind a different pigment, a common, sullen earth tone, his movements deliberate, a redirection of the gaze toward the sheer, grinding cost of the binder, the relentless materiality of the making. To name oneself was to invite the merchant’s sudden gaze, the inspection that saw not the devotion trapped in the lime, but only the price tag, the fleeting commodity. To exist on the wall was to be consumed by the praise, to be fixed—and fixed things, he knew from the stain on his own wrist, become burdens too heavy for the hand that laid them down. The silence that followed the master’s deliberate shift in hue was not empty; it was a dense, pressurized medium, the accumulated weight of every anonymous hand that had ever coaxed beauty from the porous breath of the haveli stone, leaving only the shadow of the painter against the sleeping town.
