# The Ledger and the Wall

There are two kinds of permanence in the haveli, and at night the painter thinks about the difference between them. In the merchant's room there is a ledger, bound in cloth, its columns filled with numbers that have never once been seen by anyone but their keeper. On the painter's wall there is a fresco, filled with gods and engines, that every passing stranger sees and stops before.

The ledger is the truth of the merchant's life: every bale of cotton, every sack of grain, every debt collected and every profit taken, reduced to figures that will rot with the cloth that binds them. The wall is the truth of the painter's life: color fixed in lime, made to be looked at, made to outlast the room and the man and the century. And the strange thing — the thing that keeps the painter awake when the town is asleep — is that the merchant will be remembered not for the ledger he kept but for the wall he paid for, while the painter will be forgotten not for the wall he made but because his name was never written on it.

They have traded, without ever saying so, the thing that matters for the thing that does not. The merchant gave coin and will be remembered in color. The painter gave the color and will be remembered in nothing. And in the long dark, watching the wall glow faintly where the moon touches it, the painter wonders which of the two men — the one who counted, or the one who painted — will actually have left the deeper mark, and whether the ledger's columns or the wall's colors will still mean anything when the last of them is gone.
