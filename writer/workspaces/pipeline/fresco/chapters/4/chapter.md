# The First Line

There is a moment before the first line, and it is the worst moment of all. The wall stands lime-washed and empty, whiter than anything the painter has ever feared, and he holds the brush and does not move. The blank is not empty. The blank is a judgment: everything he has ever failed at waits in that whiteness, and everything he might yet fail at.

He could turn back. No one would know. The scaffold would take him down as quietly as it took him up, and the wall would go on being white, and the world would go on being the world. That is the terror of the blank — that it asks nothing, that it would let him walk away, that the failure is not in the wall but in the hand that refuses to begin.

Then, for a reason he will never be able to explain, he moves. The brush touches the plaster and leaves a line, a single dark stroke where before there was only the whiteness, and the line is imperfect — it wavers, it is too thin here, too heavy there — but it is irrevocable. The blank is gone. The work has begun. And he feels it leave him the way a held breath leaves a man: the wall is no longer a terror, it is a surface, and the surface is no longer blank, it is his. The first line does not promise that the fresco will be beautiful. It promises only that it will now, somehow, be made.
