# The Blossom

Morning comes and finds the wall finished, and the town opens its eyes upon the blossom. They come in ones and twos — the cloth merchant on his way to the bazaar, the woman with the water jar, the children who were told not to touch — and they stop before the haveli and raise their faces to what the painter made in the dark.

The colors are still damp with the last of the night. A god rides his painted chariot; a steam engine, blue and improbable, carries its tiny passengers across a panel of ochre; the whole wall blooms like a garden that opened while everyone slept. And they praise it. Their voices rise and overlap, and the praise is honest, and it lands on the wall the way morning light lands on water — everywhere at once, and not once on the maker.

The painter stands in the shadow of the gate and watches the praise slide past his own hand. No one looks at the hand. No one asks what the hand paid. The blossom is enough for them, and why should it not be — it is the only thing they were ever meant to see. But standing there, in the applause that never finds him, he understands for the first time the exact weight of his bargain: that the blossom would be theirs, and the struggle would be his alone, and that this is what it means to make a thing the world will love without ever loving the one who made it.
