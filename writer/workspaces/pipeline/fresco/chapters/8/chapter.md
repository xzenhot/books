# The Apprentice's Question

The apprentice grinds the pigment in slow circles, the stone turning red under the weight of the mineral, and the question has been waiting in him all day. At last he asks it, not looking up from the stone.

"Why do we do it," he says, "when no one ever sees us do it? The merchant takes the credit. The wall is praised. We might as well not exist."

The painter watches the boy's hands move, and for a long time he says nothing. He wants to give the boy a good answer — something about immortality, about the color outlasting the flesh, about the honor of the craft — but every answer he reaches for turns to dust on his tongue. Because the truth is he has asked himself the same question a thousand times, and he has never once found an answer that satisfied him. He works through the night not because he has resolved the question but because the work itself has become the only answer he can bear.

And so the silence stretches between them, and the stone keeps turning, and the boy does not press him. In the silence the painter understands something he could not have said aloud: that the question is not a problem to be solved but a fire to be lived inside, and that a man can spend his whole life asking why he labors unseen and still be unable to stop laboring. The question is the craft. The answer, if it ever comes, will not be a word but the wall itself, still standing in the light, long after the one who asked is gone.
