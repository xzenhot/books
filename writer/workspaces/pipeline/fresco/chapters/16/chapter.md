The dust, fine as pulverized bone, settled on the wooden struts of the scaffold, a gray shroud against the sudden, uncompromising blaze of the midday sun. Standing in the courtyard, the heat rising from the packed earth like a slow, silent exhalation, I looked not at the painted figures of the ancestors, but at the wall itself—a palimpsest where my own unsaid things lay buried beneath the final, triumphant wash of ultramarine. Here, the lime, that ancient skin, seemed to breathe with the same slow, geologic patience as the accretion of memory; each layer, each careful application of pigment, was a deliberate act of self-editing. Did the vibrant pattern I had labored over, that intricate border meant to ward off the mundane gaze, truly capture the schisms within? Or was the pattern itself—the meticulous layering of preparatory sketch, wash, and final glaze—a perfect, polished facsimile of my own self-deception? My fingers, stained with the slick, oily residue of stabilizers, traced the edge of a painted leaf, and the gesture felt less like adoration of craft and more like the clumsy, uncertain tracing of a forgotten line in my own ledger. The façade, so admired by the passing merchants, demanded a narrative of flawless, eternal beauty. But beneath the bloom, beneath the surface sheen, the wall revealed the chemical degradation—the subtle flaking where the binder failed, the silent testimony to the sleepless vigils and the doubts that stained the substrate before the first speck of color was laid down. I was not recording a life; I was composing myself, a careful assemblage of borrowed mineral dust and the rigid structure of necessity, until the man felt less like a self and more like a highly skilled imitation held against the indifferent, bright sky.
