The house was still standing when she returned, and that was the first wrong thing.

It had no reason to be standing. She had left it seven winters ago and told no one she was coming back, and yet the door was unlocked and the lamp in the kitchen was warm, as if someone had just gone out. She stood on the step and listened. The wind moved through the rooms. Somewhere, a clock she did not remember owning began to tick.

She called her sister's name. It did not echo. It fell flat, the way a name falls when the air has learned to hold its breath.

Inside, the floor was swept. The bed was made. On the table, a cup of tea had gone cold, but the cold was fresh — not the cold of days, but the cold of an hour, maybe two. She touched it and pulled her hand back, and did not know why her heart had begun to count.

The clock stopped.

She did not turn on the lights. Some part of her knew the light would be worse than the dark, would show her what the dark was still allowing her not to see. So she stood in the doorway of the kitchen, in the failing light of an afternoon that had no business ending, and listened to the house be still around her, and waited for the sound of her sister's footsteps on the stairs.

They did not come. But something else did, from the far room — a slow, wet sound, like a mouth opening in the dark. And she understood, at last, that she had not returned to an empty house. She had only returned before the house was finished with her.
