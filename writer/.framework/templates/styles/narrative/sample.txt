The letter arrived on the last day of the rains, and nothing after it was the same.

Ami found it wedged under the door, the ink already run in places where the water had reached it, so that only half the words survived. She read what she could: a name, a place, a date that had already passed. Then she read it again, and a third time, and folded it into her pocket as if it might warm her.

She did not tell her mother. She did not tell anyone. Instead she packed a small bag, took the coins from the jar above the stove, and walked to the station while the streets were still soft and dark with rain. The train that took her was the same one she had watched leave every evening from the window, carrying strangers toward a city she had only ever seen in photographs.

Three stations in, the train stopped for an hour, and a man in the next seat told her the tracks ahead had washed out. She listened, and nodded, and did not get off. The rain went on, and the train waited, and Ami sat very still, her hand on the folded letter, her eyes on the water running down the glass.

By morning the tracks were clear. The train moved on. And the city she had only ever imagined came up slowly out of the mist, brick by brick, until it was no longer a photograph but a place with a name, and a door, and someone on the other side who did not yet know she was coming.
