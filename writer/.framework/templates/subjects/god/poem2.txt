# Sample 2 — God

## Holy Sonnet 14 ("Batter my heart, three-person'd God")

**By John Donne** (England, 1572–1631)

*Source: Holy Sonnets (c. 1609–1610; first published 1633). Public domain worldwide.*

---

Batter my heart, three-person'd God; for you
As yet but knock, breathe, shine, and seek to mend;
That I may rise, and stand, o'erthrow me, and bend
Your force, to break, blow, burn, and make me new.
I, like an usurp'd town, to another due,
Labour to admit you, but Oh, to no end;
Reason, your viceroy in me, me should defend,
But is captived, and proves weak or untrue.
Yet dearly I love you, and would be loved fain,
But am betroth'd unto your enemy:
Divorce me, untie, or break that knot again,
Take me to you, imprison me, for I,
Except you enthrall me, never shall be free,
Nor ever chaste, except you ravish me.

---

## Notes

- **Poet:** John Donne, the English metaphysical poet and later Dean of St Paul's.
- **Country:** England.
- **Era:** Early 17th century; the Holy Sonnets were written around 1609–1610.
- **Theme:** The soul's desperate plea to God — a love so fierce it asks to be broken, overthrown, and remade, because only total surrender can set the self free.
- **Contrast:** Where Tagore's Gitanjali (sample1) speaks of God as gentle, endless giving, Donne cries out for a God of force and violence. The two together show the range of the soul's address to the divine — the tender and the desperate.
