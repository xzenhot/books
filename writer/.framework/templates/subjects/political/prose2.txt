# Prose Sample 2 — Politics

## Anandamath

**By Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay** (Bengal, India, 1838–1894)

*Source: Anandamath (1882), a Bengali novel. Public domain in India; translated into many languages.*

---

It was a dark night of the year 1770. The whole of Bengal was then in the grip
of a terrible famine. The fields lay bare, the granaries were empty, and the
roads were lined with the dead and the dying. In such a night, a man was walking
alone through a forest, his face haggard with hunger, his steps unsteady.

His name was Mahendra. He had left his home, his wife, and his little daughter,
and had come out into the wilderness, driven by despair. For the land he loved
was in the hands of strangers, and the people he loved were dying of want.

As he walked, he heard, far off, the sound of singing. It was a hymn, and the
words rose and fell through the trees like a prayer. He followed the sound, and
came at last to a clearing, where a band of men, gaunt and armed, were gathered
around a fire, singing to the Mother — to the land itself, as to a goddess.

"Vande Mataram," they sang. "I bow to thee, Mother."

---

## Notes

- **Writer:** Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, the Bengali novelist who gave India its national song, "Vande Mataram."
- **Country:** Bengal, India.
- **Era:** Late 19th century; the novel was published in 1882.
- **Theme:** The nation as mother — the land itself worshipped as a goddess, and the call to rise against colonial rule in her name.
- **Contrast:** Where Orwell's Animal Farm (prose1) warns against the corruption of power, Bankim Chandra's novel is the source of the dream itself — the land as sacred mother, and the struggle for her freedom as a holy war.
