# Sample 1 — Philosophy

## The Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám (excerpt)

**By Omar Khayyám** (Persia, 1048–1131), translated by Edward FitzGerald

*Source: Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám, translated by Edward FitzGerald (1859). Public domain worldwide.*

---

The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ,
Moves on: nor all thy Piety nor Wit
Shall lure it back to cancel half a Line,
Nor all thy Tears wash out a Word of it.

And that inverted Bowl they call the Sky,
Whereunder crawling coop'd we live and die,
Lift not your hands to It for help—for It
As impotently moves as you or I.

With Earth's first Clay They did the Last Man knead,
And there of the Last Harvest sow'd the Seed:
And the first Morning of Creation wrote
What the Last Dawn of Reckoning shall read.

---

## Notes

- **Poet:** Omar Khayyám, the Persian mathematician, astronomer, and poet, in Edward FitzGerald's celebrated English translation.
- **Country:** Persia (Iran).
- **Era:** 11th–12th century; FitzGerald's translation was published in 1859.
- **Theme:** Fate, mortality, and the limits of human will — the poem's meditation on a universe that moves on regardless of our piety or tears.
- **Contrast:** This is philosophy as resignation before fate. It pairs with Tagore's Gitanjali 35 (sample2), which answers the same questions of freedom and destiny with a very different, more hopeful vision.
