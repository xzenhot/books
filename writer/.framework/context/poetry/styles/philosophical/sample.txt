What does it mean to say that we have "lost" a thing — a home, a person, a time?

First the term. To lose is not simply to be without; it is to be without something that was once ours, and to know that it was ours. The stone never loses the mountain. Only the one who possessed can lose, and only because possession is a way of saying: this, for a while, belonged to me.

But here the premise weakens. Did the time truly belong to us, or did we belong to the time? We speak of "my childhood" as if it were a room we owned and then vacated, when perhaps it is closer to the truth to say that childhood held us, briefly, and then released us into a world we did not choose.

Test it against experience. The grief we feel for what is gone is not the grief of losing a possession; it is the grief of ceasing to be the person to whom that thing belonged. We mourn the self that is no longer available, not the object that is no longer present.

So the question turns. What we call loss may be only the felt distance between who we are and who we were — a distance no possession, recovered, could ever close.

And if that is so, then what we are really asking, whenever we say we have lost something, is not "where did it go?" but "who have I become?" — a question that does not answer so much as open.
