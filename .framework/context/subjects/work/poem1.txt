# Sample 1 — Work

## The Village Blacksmith

**By Henry Wadsworth Longfellow** (United States, 1807–1882)

*Source: Ballads and Other Poems (1842). Public domain worldwide.*

---

Under a spreading chestnut-tree
The village smithy stands;
The smith, a mighty man is he,
With large and sinewy hands;
And the muscles of his brawny arms
Are strong as iron bands.

His hair is crisp, and black, and long,
His face is like the tan;
His brow is wet with honest sweat,
He earns whate'er he can,
And looks the whole world in the face,
For he owes not any man.

Week in, week out, from morn till night,
You can hear his bellows blow;
You can hear him swing his heavy sledge,
With measured beat and slow,
Like a sexton ringing the village bell,
When the evening sun is low.

---

## Notes

- **Poet:** Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, the American poet.
- **Country:** United States.
- **Era:** Mid-19th century; the poem was published in 1842.
- **Theme:** The dignity of labor — the blacksmith who earns what he can, owes no man, and looks the whole world in the face, his work a steady, honest rhythm.
- **Contrast:** This is work as honest pride. It pairs with Tagore's Gitanjali 11 (sample2), which sees work as a form of worship — the laborer who leaves off his singing to till the hard ground.
