A thing is understood when it can be explained in plain words. This is the working principle of exposition: the writer's task is not to impress but to make clear.

Begin with the claim. State it once, simply, so the reader knows where the road leads. Then order the material. Each fact should follow from the one before it, as one step follows another on a stair, so that the reader never has to leap.

Define as you go. A word that carries a technical meaning should be given its meaning at the moment it first appears, not three paragraphs later. For example, consider how a river forms: rain falls on high ground, gathers into channels, and seeks the sea. Each term — channel, gather, seek — is already doing its work; none requires translation.

Anchor abstraction in instance. A general rule is only as convincing as the concrete case that shows it working. And when the last point is made, return to the first. The reader who began in the dark should end in the light, holding the whole shape of the idea in one clear view.
