The Right Of The Flower

When right throws away every ledger of ownership and entitlement and announces its existence in a wild rhythm, neither the state nor society awakens first; what awakens is a thirsty geography. It becomes an ancient assertion. What we call a claim has no treaty or partition in the depths of the forest. There, right means a blade of sunlight, a handful of rainwater. But when the traders of that light spread their branches so wide that they shade others into darkness, the misted jungle of right begins.

The question is not for a teacher; it is for absorbed nature itself: who truly grants a right? These ancient trees, these vines and shrubs, to whom do they stretch their hands? Is the forest a separate giver? It is, in truth, the collective being of millions of roots and fallen leaves. The wildflower is the forest, and the thorn of the acacia is also the forest. It is its own owner and its own beggar. It asks the soil for the right to open its petals, while that same soil is held together by its roots.

Yet in the grammar of the human world, we have learned only to spread our branches. We want to seize the sky in our fists. We want to drain every juice from the earth and rise as the noble tree. Is this what we call right? Or is it a parasitic vine that sucks the blood of its host and then declares itself a great tree?

In this forest, a thousand languages rustle. There are countless leaf-patterns, countless wild colors of movement. But if each being mistakes its own shade for final truth, if each memorizes only the grammar of taking, does the forest remain a forest? It becomes a jungle of disorder. No one takes responsibility for flowering; no one drops leaves to enrich the soil. Everyone receives; no one gives. Everyone hunts, yet no one serves the ecosystem. This is a forest where no law moves, only the arrogant cry of "I, I, I."

In the classroom of civilization we memorized the definition of right. But where is the student on the last bench who will ask, "Sir, do I owe nothing to the soil on which I stand?" In this questionless jungle everyone is misplaced. No one knows their roots, yet everyone wants to tear another's branch in the name of right. Amid this disorder, the wild datura of Palamau and the vine of Awadh stand lonely. They have seen how the language born from ancient need has become only complaint, insult, and demand.

Who bears responsibility for holding together this thousandfold greenness, this kingdom of leaves? If everyone bends as they please and breaks the disciplined rhythm of the gathering, can the great forest bear its own weight? When right becomes only a fence around private interest, it is no longer a stairway toward the sky; it becomes a self-destructive trap.

When autumn comes deep into the forest, the night jasmine gives its fragrance silently. It does not consult a calendar before claiming its right. It knows that blooming is its nature, and falling is its responsibility. But we sit in the concrete jungle and fight over who can extract more than the other. This is a wilderness where the tiger kills the deer not from hunger, but to test the strength of its paw.

The day lengthens. Red-soil villages turn to stone, and our jungle of rights grows thicker and darker. There is no water here, no care, only the high-rise desire of competition. Before surrendering to that competition, let us take one deep breath and think about the meaning of our own existence.

History has become a cliche here. In this duel between right and responsibility, the forest is tired. Yet when night deepens and the moon sinks, something returns in the primary dark. From zero, counting begins again. What has bloomed remains.

The true right of the forest lies in blooming, not in covering others. A right is not a slogan or a clever link; it is an absorbed discipline. To survive in this forest, one must learn to become a giver. Otherwise the jungle of rights will one day devour itself.

The flower must bloom to answer the responsibility of its own existence, not to steal another's right. That is the oldest and only law of the forest.
